Field guide

The examples use the starting format with 3 exponent bits and 4 mantissa bits. The sign stays at 0, so every finite value is nonnegative.

How does the exponent bias work?

With e exponent bits, the bias is 2e − 1 − 1. Subtracting this offset lets an unsigned bit field describe both negative and positive powers of two.

For 3 bits, the bias is 3. The stored exponent 100 is 4 in decimal, so the actual exponent is 4 − 3 = 1. The scale factor is 21 = 2. The all-zero and all-one exponent fields have special rules.

How do the mantissa bits become a number?

Read the stored bits after the binary point. Their weights are 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, and so on. Each 1 adds its weight. A normal number has an implicit leading 1, giving the full significand.

With 0 100 1010, the significand is 1.10102 = 1 + 1/2 + 1/8 = 1.625. Multiply by the scale factor of 2 to get 3.25. Each time the exponent increases by one, this scale factor doubles, and so does the distance between neighboring ticks.

What happens near zero?

An all-zero exponent gives an implicit leading 0 and fixes the exponent at 1 − bias. A nonzero mantissa then represents a subnormal (denormalized) number. An all-zero mantissa in this exponent field gives zero.

The smallest positive bit pattern, 0 000 0001, gives (1/16) × 2−2 = 1/64. Subnormals rise in steps of 1/64. From the largest subnormal, 15/64, one more step reaches the smallest normal, 16/64 = 0.25. These evenly spaced values between zero and the smallest normal provide gradual underflow.

How does rounding choose a tick?

The input can fall anywhere between two ticks. The format stores the nearer tick. At an exact midpoint, choose the value whose last significand bit is 0. This is round to nearest, ties to even.

Try 1 and its next tick, 1.0625. Their midpoint is 1.03125, which rounds to 1 because its mantissa ends in 0. Just above that midpoint, the nearer tick is 1.0625.

At the top of this format, the last two ticks are 15 and 15.5. Extending that spacing would put the next tick at 16. At 15.75, halfway between 15.5 and 16, rounding carries into an exponent too large to store. Inputs of 15.75 or more round to +∞.

Where are epsilon, the smallest normal, and the largest finite value?

Machine epsilon is the gap from 1 to the next larger representable value. With m mantissa bits it equals 2−m. Here the gap is 1.0625 − 1 = 0.0625.

Smallest positive normal. Set the exponent field to 1 and the mantissa to all zeros. The pattern 0 001 0000 gives 1 × 2−2 = 0.25.

Largest finite value. Use the highest exponent field below all ones, then fill the mantissa with ones. The pattern 0 110 1111 gives (1 + 15/16) × 23 = 15.5.

Which patterns mean infinity and NaN?

An all-ones exponent reserves the pattern for a special value. With a zero mantissa, 0 111 0000 means positive infinity (+∞). With any nonzero mantissa, such as 0 111 0001, it means NaN (“not a number”). An undefined operation such as 0 ÷ 0 produces NaN, which has no position on the number line.

How do 32-bit and 64-bit floats compare?

IEEE 754 binary32 and binary64 use the same field structure and special cases. More exponent bits extend the range. More mantissa bits fit more ticks between adjacent powers of two.

The implicit leading 1 gives normal values 24 significant binary digits in binary32 and 53 in binary64.
